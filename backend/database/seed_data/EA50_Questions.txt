HARD CONCEPT 1: #rightproblem
================================================================================
Definition: Characterize a complex problem in detail to really understand it.

Question 1:
Q: A city proposes "add more buses" to cut congestion without describing current traffic flows or success metrics. What's missing?
A) Goal state
B) Initial state and measurement plan ✓
C) Scale only
D) Nothing—solution first is fine
Explanation: #rightproblem requires a clear baseline and how success will be measured before proposing solutions.

Question 2:
Q: Which goal state avoids presupposing a solution?
A) Ban cars downtown
B) Achieve average bus wait time ≤ 7 minutes by Q4 ✓
C) Build a new subway line
D) Switch traffic lights to adaptive control
Explanation: A measurable goal describes outcomes, not specific interventions.

Question 3:
Q: You listed initial state, goal state, obstacles, and scale, but not likely consequences if nothing changes. What should you add?
A) Constraint list
B) Future implications of the problem ✓
C) Budget section only
D) SWOT table
Explanation: Robust characterization includes likely consequences and implications.

Question 4:
Q: A hospital aims to "reduce patient complaints" but doesn't specify which types of complaints or current frequency. Why is this problematic?
A) The goal is too ambitious
B) Without baseline data and specificity, success cannot be measured ✓
C) Complaints are not important
D) Solutions should come before measurement
Explanation: Characterizing the right problem requires quantifying the initial state and defining specific success criteria.

Question 5:
Q: A startup identifies "low user engagement" as their problem but hasn't examined which features users actually use or why they leave. What critical step is missing?
A) Defining the goal state
B) Characterizing the initial state through data analysis ✓
C) Listing constraints
D) Brainstorming solutions
Explanation: Understanding the current state in detail—including user behavior patterns—is essential before defining solutions.

Question 6:
Q: Two teams tackle food waste. Team A defines the goal as "implement composting programs." Team B defines it as "reduce cafeteria waste by 40% within 6 months." Which follows #rightproblem better?
A) Team A, because composting is a proven solution
B) Team B, because they specify measurable outcomes without presupposing one solution ✓
C) Both equally, since goals are subjective
D) Neither, since waste reduction is too vague
Explanation: Outcome-focused goals leave room for multiple solution paths; solution-focused goals prematurely narrow options.

Question 7:
Q: A school says "students are failing math" but hasn't identified whether the issue is foundational gaps, teaching methods, or motivation. What should they do first?
A) Hire more tutors immediately
B) Characterize the problem by analyzing failure patterns and root causes ✓
C) Switch to a new curriculum
D) Increase homework assignments
Explanation: Understanding the nature and scope of failures is essential before selecting interventions.

Question 8:
Q: A company characterizes a problem with initial state, goal state, and obstacles—but omits constraints like budget and regulatory requirements. What risk does this create?
A) The problem becomes too easy to solve
B) Solutions may be infeasible or illegal ✓
C) Obstacles will disappear
D) The initial state will change

Question 9:
Q: A nonprofit states their problem as "need more funding" rather than "cannot serve 200+ families on waitlist due to capacity limits." Which framing better applies #rightproblem?
A) The first, because funding is always the core issue
B) The second, because it describes the real problem and goal state without presupposing one solution ✓
C) Both are equally valid
D) Neither, since nonprofits should not define problems
Explanation: Reframing from "need X resource" to "cannot achieve Y outcome" opens up multiple solution pathways.

Question 10:
Q: An urban planning team characterizes traffic congestion by documenting peak hours, bottleneck locations, commuter origins, and economic costs. What component would complete their characterization?
A) A list of potential solutions
B) Projected consequences if congestion continues unchecked ✓
C) A detailed budget breakdown
D) Stakeholder voting results
Explanation: Full problem characterization includes implications—what happens if the problem persists.

Definition: Identify and apply constraint satisfaction as a way to solve problems.

Question 1:
Q: Budget is fixed this quarter. How should you treat it?
A) Constraint ✓
B) Obstacle
C) Neither
D) Both—depends on mood
Explanation: Non-negotiable boundaries are constraints in the current framing.

Question 2:
Q: Which action demonstrates constraint satisfaction?
A) Brainstorming without limits
B) Selecting a schedule that fits room capacity, instructor availability, and time windows ✓
C) Expanding scope until a solution appears
D) Copying a competitor's plan
Explanation: Constraint satisfaction finds solutions that meet all constraints simultaneously.

Question 3:
Q: You misclassified a reparable equipment failure as a constraint. What's the fix?
A) Keep it a constraint
B) Reframe as an obstacle and plan remediation ✓
C) Remove it entirely
D) Treat it as a success metric
Explanation: Solvable items are obstacles; treat with actions, not as fixed bounds.

Question 4:
Q: A team must schedule a conference with 5 constraints: venue availability, speaker schedules, budget limits, accessibility requirements, and catering lead times. What approach applies #constraints?
A) Pick one constraint and ignore the rest
B) Find a solution that simultaneously satisfies all 5 constraints ✓

D) Prioritize constraints and violate lower-priority ones

A) Obstacle, because construction can be rescheduled or phased differently ✓

Question 6:

A) Treat preferences as constraints to simplify
B) Satisfy hard constraints first; optimize preferences within feasible solutions ✓

D) All constraints are equally negotiable

A) Additional preferences like taste or nutrition balance ✓

Question 8:

Question 2:
Q: Which is required for a solid gap analysis?
A) Listing one familiar option
B) Deep evaluation of multiple existing solutions against requirements/constraints ✓

Question 3:
Q: Using a proven same-domain solution with slight tweaks typically reflects:
A) #analogies
